# Narrative and slide writing

## Build the larger story

Draft the argument in prose before producing slides. A typical path is objective, relevant context, brand/competition diagnosis, audience tension, strategic opportunity, media roles, tangible executions, investment, measurement and next decisions. Adapt it to the brief; do not force every chapter into every deck.

Ask what the reader needs to understand before the next recommendation can make sense. Place audience profiling before platform mapping, media architecture before detailed delivery, and a partner rationale before its execution details. Keep the opening focused. Give material partners sufficient room without violating the requested count. Combine slides when they serve the same thought and readability improves.

Treat a requested count as inclusive of cover and appendix unless explicitly stated otherwise. Resolve the scope before production. Put supporting depth in an appendix or separate research pack only when allowed. Do not quietly exceed the limit.

## Blueprint every slide

Maintain stable slide IDs and these fields: chapter, purpose, reader question, header, takeaway, on-slide copy, claim/source references, visual/asset plan, metric definitions, disclosures, notes, next-slide connection, dependencies and revision locks. The JSON template is a working blueprint, not a deck generator.

Give each slide one principal job. Choose its visual to carry that job: evidence comparison, cohort profile, journey, map, chart, placement mockup, storyboard, timeline or economic model. Do not turn every slide into the same table or panel grid.

Read the header sequence without body copy. It should convey the argument and make each chapter's role understandable. A useful factual setup title is acceptable when a stronger conclusion would overstate the evidence.

## Write value-conveying headers

Draft the takeaway before the body. Make the header tell the reader what matters, with certainty proportionate to the evidence. These examples illustrate treatment only; use them only when actual evidence supports the claim.

| Topic label | More useful treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Competitor analysis | Competitors repeat the same speed and price claims |
| Target audience | Homeowners and specifiers need different reasons to choose |
| Partner plan | Community placements connect the brand to daily home routines |
| Hero idea | The product becomes the control inside the ad experience |
| Budget scenarios | The larger budget adds regional video to the core markets |
| Measurement | Qualified outcomes determine where investment scales |

Avoid headlines with invented causality, guaranteed impact or unsupported numbers. The body and visual must substantiate the header.

## Use the deck voice

Write professionally, informatively, directly and confidently. Use familiar paid-media language and explain essential acronyms once. Make commercial meaning explicit. Use short complete thoughts rather than piles of keywords. Replace vague words such as 'impactful engagement' with the audience, action and purpose.

Let confidence come from clear choices and evidence. Keep wit in brand-appropriate creative concepts when useful; do not default to a quirky personal voice in analytical sections. Do not insert the skill creator's biography or attribution into client decks.

Keep only the information needed to understand or support the point. Remove filler subtitles, repetitive takeaway boxes and repeated explanations. Use selective emphasis for the decisive finding. Keep decision-changing caveats visible. Speaker notes can hold detail but must not hide the reasoning a reader needs to understand the recommendation without a presenter.
